1. False Positives — The Analyzer Finds A Root, But The Wrong One

The most common failure mode is a spelling collision: the analyzer successfully strips the affixes, but lands on the wrong root because two valid roots have the same surface form after stripping.

Example: mamasa — The engine strips the transitive prefix, which nasalizes the stem-initial consonant (a rule documented by Forschner and confirmed by the Swarthmore transducer). The resulting core is masa. Under the nasalization rules, an initial m- can represent an original p-, b-, or v-. Unfortunately, the dictionary contains both basa ("to read") and pasa ("rotten"), both of which are equally short and equally valid candidates. Without a way to know which is more likely, the engine can easily parse a word meaning "reading" as if it comes from the root "rotten." I have since added a priority system that prefers the parent entry's own subentry list, which helps considerably — but it does not solve all cases.

Example: kaka- words — Words like kakal ("still/yet"), kakau ("epilepsy"), and kakat ("pull/drag") were initially misparsed because the engine eagerly interpreted ka- as a CV-reduplication prefix, stripping it to produce false bases like kal, kau, or kat, none of which exist. I have since added a guard that checks the full word in the dictionary first before attempting any reduplication stripping.

Example: kosunduvo and kobuatano — This is a systematic error I want to raise with you specifically, because I believe I now understand what is happening — but I would like you to confirm it.

Both words are abstract nouns, but the analyzer incorrectly labels their trailing -o as the verbal suffix -o ("imperative patient focus"). This is wrong because ko- and -o together form the nominalizing circumfix ko-...-o, which creates abstract nouns from root stems — not verb commands. The issue is that the analyzer does not distinguish between these two homophonous suffixes: the verbal -o (imperative) and the nominal -o (abstract noun nominalizer).

The morphophonological behavior is also consistent with Forschner's grammar. In kobuatano, the root buatan ends in a consonant, so the suffix -o is appended directly with no change. In kosunduvo, the root sundu ends in the high vowel /u/, and Rungus grammar inserts a /v/ glide to bridge the two adjacent vowels before adding -o: ko- + sundu + o → ko-sundu-vo → kosunduvo. I found the same rule applied consistently elsewhere in the dictionary: anaru ("long") → konoruvo ("length"), which follows the exact same pattern (anaru ends in /u/, glide inserted, suffix -o appended).

The fix for the analyzer would be: when the prefix ko- is present and the trailing morpheme is -o (or -vo after a vowel-final root), treat the suffix as the nominalizing -o, not the imperative -o. I am fairly confident this analysis is correct based on the pattern in the data, but I would appreciate your confirmation — particularly on whether the ko-...-o circumfix is fully productive in Rungus, and whether there are other high vowels (like /i/) that trigger the same glide insertion.

2. False Negatives — The Analyzer Fails to Parse or Connect a Word to its Root

Example: kopioondusan — This word appears 209 times across the nine books but initially failed to parse because of a vowel harmony shift. The root is actually ondos ("to guess/think/suspect"), but under the influence of the suffix -an, the second vowel /o/ shifts to /u/, resulting in ondus. When combined with the intensive prefix kopio- ("extremely/truly"), it forms kopioondusan ("extremely suspecting / full of assumptions"). Since the dictionary only contains the base ondos, the analyzer initially failed to recognize the root until I implemented vowel de-harmonization (reversing u to o) to reconstruct the canonical form. I have found dozens of similar cases of complex phonetic shifts. To address this, I have been working on a "Human-in-the-Loop" (HITL) workflow where an LLM examines the surrounding sentence context and pre-fills a spreadsheet with educated guesses about the root and meaning, which a human reviewer can then confirm or correct.

As a small test of this workflow, I exported the top 500 unanalyzed words from the corpus. I have attached the pre-filled spreadsheet (reviewed_testv1.csv) along with the raw list (top500before_review.csv) for you to look over. The AI performs surprisingly well on loanwords (like kampong and bandar) but struggles, understandably, with native Rungus vocabulary — which is exactly where your expertise would be invaluable.
